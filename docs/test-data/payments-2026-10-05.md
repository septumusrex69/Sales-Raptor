# A payment file to test the split

`payments-2026-10-05.csv` beside this file is an FNB trust-account export in the format Finance →
**Import a bank statement** reads. Twelve lines: eight payments designed to land on a particular
part of the 50/50 rule, and four more that exist to prove the importer tells the difference between
a receipt and everything else.

**Import it from Finance → Back office → Import a bank statement.** Nothing is written until you
have seen the preview.

The first: the interest column was computed on the **capital only** and came out the same 1.2587%
of capital on every row. The engine runs interest on the **outstanding balance** — capital, fees
and interest already run, less payments — and each fee joins that balance on the day it was
incurred, so three accounts with R380 of capital and different fees now carry three different
figures (R5,09, R5,62 and R5,28) rather than R4,78 apiece. The engine is the one calculation; this
table now quotes it rather than a second opinion about it.

The second: **RRC00005 is at its in duplum ceiling.** R437,01 of fees ran, and NCA s103(5) stops
interest, fees and costs together exceeding the capital outstanding, so only R380,00 of them can
ever be recovered — R57,01 can not. That difference used to be invisible; it is now a column of
its own on the approval screen, headed "fees ceiling refuses", and it is the reason the firm's own
formula has to read **a − ceiling − b = available** rather than a − b. Written their way exactly,
the check would have cried wolf on this account while its arithmetic was perfectly correct.

## How the split actually works

Four steps, in this order. Everything below is this rule applied to a different sized payment.

1. **The receipt fee is charged first, so the payment can pay it.** Annexure B item 9: 10% of the
   payment, capped at R610 excluding VAT, plus VAT. It joins costs *before* the split.
2. **The payment is cut in half.** Half B takes the odd cent, so the halves always add back.
3. **Half A pays interest first, then costs.** Whatever half A cannot spend — because interest and
   costs are already settled — **rolls over to capital**.
4. **Half B, plus the roll, pays capital.** If capital clears and money is still left, it goes back
   to any remaining interest and costs; anything after that is held as a **credit** and is never
   paid over to the client.

**Five rows take exactly half to fees and half to capital** — the 50/50 rule in full effect.
**Three take less than half to fees** (RRC00002, RRC00006, RRC00007), because everything the fees
side had to pay was settled and the rest rolled to capital: the firm's "it's 50-50 unless the fees
are less than 50% of the payment, then the rest is allocated to the capital."

**AND FOUR OF THEM PAY NOTHING TOWARDS THIS PAYMENT'S OWN RECEIPT FEE.** RRC00001, 4, 5 and 8 show
a dash in that column, and it is not a bug — it is the thing the three-way split was built to make
visible. The costs pool is settled **oldest fee first**, the receipt fee this payment raises is the
newest fee on the account, and on a small payment the Annexure B backlog takes the whole costs
half before its turn comes. RRC00001 has R324,31 of older fees against a costs half of R139,72, so
its own R34,50 receipt fee is still outstanding afterwards. Before the split, a single "receipt
fee" column said R34,50 and the firm had no way to tell that none of it had been paid.

**RRC00002 is the R1 938,60 "FULL AND FINAL" and it leaves R22,77 of capital behind.** That is the
settlement-figure question still open from before: the quoted figure adds a receipt fee computed on
the **balance**, but the fee actually charged is 10% of the **payment**, which is larger. Whether
to gross the quote up or write the R22,77 off is the firm's decision and nothing here makes it.

## The four lines that are not payments

They are in the file on purpose: the importer has to tell them apart, and the preview should say so
before anything is written.

| Line | What it is | What should happen |
|---|---|---|
| `CR.INT.RATE   4,25000` · R0.00 | The bank's interest-rate notice | Counted as a **note**. Not a receipt. |
| `CAPITEC L SOLOMONS` · R485.00 | A credit with a depositor's name and no reference | Lands in **Unallocated receipts** for somebody to place. About a quarter of real receipts look like this. |
| `FNB APP PAYMENT TO RINDA ROO COMPANY` · −R962.50 | Money going **out** — a remittance | A **debit**. Importing it as a receipt would credit a debtor with money the firm paid away. |
| `##BANK CHARGE` · −R1.00 | A bank charge | A debit. |

Expect the preview to read: **9 credits · 8 with a reference · 1 without · 2 debits · 1 note.**

## After the import, worth checking

- **Commission** is 30% on this client. It is earned on what reaches the **client**, not on the
  whole receipt — so check it against the capital column, not the payment.
- **The money position** on each account: capital taken, costs taken, costs that cannot be taken,
  and the in duplum flag on RRC00005.
- **Re-upload the same file.** Nothing should double. Every line carries a key made of its date,
  amount, description and its position among identical lines, so a statement that overlaps a
  previous one imports only what is new.
